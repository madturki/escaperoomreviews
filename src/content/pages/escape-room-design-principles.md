Start here if you are deciding what a room is for. The links below are the pieces designers hand each other: why the room exists, how players move through it, and the habits that make a design feel cheap.

## Safety

Emergency equipment is not a prop. Room Escape Artist's note on [emergency equipment](https://roomescapeartist.com/2025/05/25/emergency-equipment-arent-game-mechanics/) is the short version: do not hide, cover, or build puzzles out of extinguishers, alarms, or exits.

Their older list of [clichés and safety tips](https://roomescapeartist.com/2016/06/15/escape-room-top-10-cliches-top-10-safety-tips/) pairs the same warning with the themes and set pieces that have already been done to death.

## Why the room exists

Scott Nicholson's [Ask Why](http://scottnicholson.com/pubs/askwhy.pdf) is the paper people still send when a design has puzzles but no reason. His companion piece maps rooms onto the [eight types of fun](https://scottnicholson.com/pubs/funescape.pdf).

Markus Wiemker, Errol Elumir, and Adam Clare wrote an early account of the [core concepts of escape room design](https://thecodex.ca/wp-content/uploads/2016/08/00511Wiemker-et-al-Paper-Escape-Room-Games.pdf). It is still the cleanest overview of what these games are made of.

Alastair Aitchison lines the format up against older definitions of a game in [what counts as a game](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/applying-videogame-theory-to-escape-rooms-the-definition-of-a-quot-game-quot-), and sorts the people in the room in his [taxonomy of player types](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/applying-videogame-theory-to-escape-rooms-a-taxonomy-of-er-player-types).

## The world and the loop

Errol Elumir's [mimesis and diegesis](https://thecodex.ca/mimesis-vs-diegesis-in-escape-rooms/) is about making the puzzle and the answer belong to the same world. His piece on the [player loop](https://thecodex.ca/how-escape-rooms-do-difficulty-wrong-the-player-loop/) is about how difficulty actually feels minute to minute, and how a room wastes that loop.

Room Escape Artist's [design basics](https://roomescapeartist.com/2015/03/20/room-design-basics/) is the short field guide. Their [glossary](https://roomescapeartist.com/2018/09/05/escape-room-glossary/) is the shared vocabulary, and the archived [Bad Puzzle Lexicon](https://web.archive.org/web/20201126002558/https://www.immersophenia.co.uk/bad-puzzle-lexicon/) names the failures designers already recognize.

[Interesting themes, and the boring ones](https://roomescapeartist.com/2018/09/23/interesting-boring-escape-room-themes-design-tips/) is their advice on stepping past the default set dressing. [Split teams](https://roomescapeartist.com/2019/03/03/split-team-regrouping-problem-escape-rooms/) covers the specific problem of a group that divides and then cannot get back together.

## Steering players

Alastair Aitchison writes about [dramatic structure](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/applying-dramatic-structure-to-escape-room-game-narratives) and about [guiding what players can see and touch](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/-quot-i-can-t-see-what-i-have-to-do-quot---guiding-player-interactions-in-escape-room-games) when the next step is invisible.

[Signposting](https://gamedevfocus.blogspot.com/2014/12/signposting-tips-and-tricks.html?m=1) is the same idea from videogames: point people down the path without announcing it. Escape The Review has a practical note on [using UV well](https://escapethereview.co.uk/how-to-use-uv-well/), which is where a lot of rooms hide the path and then lose the players.

Raph Koster's [trust spectrum](https://www.raphkoster.com/2018/03/16/the-trust-spectrum/) is about what a player will accept from a game. Graham Nelson's [Bill of Player's Rights](https://archive.org/details/TheCraftOfAdventure/page/n5), from *The Craft of Adventure*, was written for text adventures. The rights still read as a checklist for a fair room.

## Shape, hints, and the ending

Stephen Ng writes about [beginnings, middles, and ending rather than stopping](https://medium.com/@stephenng/escape-rooms-that-end-1832cd1457d2). Rachel Pendergrass's notes on [puzzle quests](https://docs.google.com/document/d/17agq76y86Q1SkBj7Q0Y3d5Nzjau6DaC5uh_6GJLLz6Y/edit) are about giving that shape some flow.

Richard Burns collected the [hint styles](https://roomescapeartist.com/2021/09/17/my-favorite-escape-room-hinting-styles/) he actually likes. The talk on [hints as fun insurance](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vqM-NsGDSv8) is the longer argument for building the hint system on purpose. Mink Ette's [talk on puzzles](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=T8rabfK67Yw) sits in the same cluster.

Matthew Stein argues for [making failure fun](https://roomescapeartist.com/2025/07/22/make-failure-fun/), and for questioning whether a room needs a fail state. A companion piece asks you to [be careful with player choice](https://roomescapeartist.com/2025/07/20/escape-rooms-player-choice/). Richard Burns, again, on the value of players feeling seen: the [idea](https://roomescapeartist.com/2025/06/29/feeling-seen-escape-rooms/), then [Hope End](https://roomescapeartist.com/2025/07/06/escape-room-players-feel-seen-hope-end/) and [Dolores Wright](https://roomescapeartist.com/2025/07/13/escape-room-players-feel-seen-dolores-wright/) as worked examples.

## Longer references

Jesse Schell's [*The Art of Game Design*](https://www.amazon.com/The-Art-Game-Design-Edition/dp/1466598646) is a general game-design book. The puzzle section is the part escape-room designers mark up.

Laura E. Hall's [*Planning Your Escape*](https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/Planning-Your-Escape/L-E-Hall/9781982140342) is a history of the format as much as a hint book. The chapter on solving a puzzle doubles as a list of ways to build one.

Jesse McGatha's [presentation on interactive events](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1FqJMHon4q4uReDQQIf_HKojarX0IGbs9/view?usp=sharing) is short and dense. Chris Waters's [Architect's Guide to Building an Adventure](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1zFGiyUR1ndOewsb2bA5rJZDxpUMLVxA2ro290n2htOc/edit) is the longer workbook from Constructed Adventures.

[*Escape Rooms and Other Immersive Experiences in the Library*](https://www.amazon.com/Escape-Rooms-Immersive-Experiences-Library/dp/0838917674) is aimed at library builds, and a lot of the design advice travels.

Shannon McDowell's work on cultural bias is worth reading as a set: the [research paper](https://press.etc.cmu.edu/file/download/1136/8ad7f311-fbce-44cc-bef6-b978ad95858f), the essay on [minimizing that bias](https://analoggamestudies.org/2022/01/minimizing-cultural-bias-in-escape-rooms/), two talks ([one](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HbniZE0f6jM), [two](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Da3aubBtvYU)), and Nicholson's paper on [cultural bias in remote rooms](https://scottnicholson.com/pubs/culturalbiasremote.pdf).
